
Another outstanding civilization that contributed to the development of Western values was Rome, located in what is now Italy. In 509 BC the Romans set up a republic - a government without a king or queen. Rome’s Republic had branches of government that included representative democracy. Branches included two consuls as executives, a senate for legislature, and Praetors who acted as judicial officials. These branches worked to share power and to represent the people. When the republic declined, nearly 500 years later, an emperor took control. The Roman Empire achieved something unique in history. It became a great power not only by conquering other lands but also by bringing the conquered peoples into the system.

While the Greeks had lived in small city-states, each governed by different laws, the Romans controlled an enormous amount of territory. They allowed conquered people to live according to their traditions in many areas, but they also tried to create a system of laws based on reason and justice, and protect people and their property. This applied throughout the Western World.

**Natural Law:** A very important feature of Roman law was the idea of natural law. As you have read, early Greek philosophers declared that the rules of nature could be discovered through careful observation. Natural law, as the Romans understood it, held that there are, in nature, certain rational ideas and standards that apply to all people at all times. The people were introduced to this idea by the Stoics, who were Greek philosophers. The Stoics believed that all people were born with the capacity to reason. Not everyone has the capacity to reason to the same degree, but ALL human beings could still reason.

Since reason was common to all peoples - Greek and non-Greek alike, slave and free, rich and poor - all people were basically equal. Since human beings are fundamentally alike, said the Stoics, they are all subjects to the same moral laws and principles. And the laws governing society cannot violate this idea. This principle underlies the idea that people have certain natural rights that they are born with, and that no legitimate government can take away.

**Citizenship - Ancient and Modern** - In the most distant provinces of the ancient Roman Empire, a person was proud to be able to say: “I am a Roman citizen.” Roman citizenship was kind of an invisible shield of respect. It meant that the citizen was protected by the laws of the great Roman Empire. Since the days of the Republic, Rome had extended citizenship to conquered territories and peoples. Finding that it was a good way to win people’s allegiance, Roman emperors continued the practice. By about AD 212, most free men and women throughout the Empire were citizens, and they were expected to fight for Rome.

The idea of basic “citizenship” like many of our basic ideas about government began with the ancient Greek city-states and the Roman Republic. Before this, people simply were considered subjects under a ruler or a king. When they began to have both rights and responsibilities in their community, people became “citizens”. As modern states have developed, the term citizen became common again. All modern nations - not just democracies - have citizens, though their rights and responsibilities vary. In general, citizens are expected to be loyal to the nation, obey its laws, pay taxes, and perhaps give military service. The government in turn, is supposed to protect its citizens.
